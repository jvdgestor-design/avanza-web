Family of a Spanish citizen: your taxes in Spain

The residence authorisation for relatives of Spanish citizens lets you live and work here; it does not decide whether you are tax resident. The route is summarised briefly below; the rest of this guide covers residence, joint returns and the special regime.

The residence route in brief

RD 1155/2024 provides a specific residence authorisation for family members of Spanish citizens where the applicant is not an EU, EEA or Swiss national. Spouses, partners, children, ascendants and other categories have different conditions, and a family relationship alone is not the full test. Being related to a Spanish citizen does not always mean applying through arraigo or general family reunification.

There are separate procedures depending on whether the Spanish citizen and the relative are in Spain or abroad. Filing alone does not authorise work. Once granted, the authorisation permits residence and employed or self-employed work throughout Spain while its conditions remain met; the usual duration is five years, or the Spanish citizen’s intended residence period if shorter.

RD 1155/2024 · Art. 93 · Art. 94 · Art. 95 · Art. 97 · RD 316/2026

Three rules that decide the family’s taxes

Each person, each year

Residence is assessed person by person and calendar year by calendar year, under the 183-day and economic-interests tests.

IRPF Act · Art. 9.1

The family presumption

A spouse not legally separated and dependent minor children, both living in Spain, create a presumption of residence that can be rebutted.

IRPF Act · Art. 9.1

Joint return

Only for the family unit at 31 December, and only if every member is an IRPF taxpayer.

IRPF Act · Arts. 82–83

The residence card does not decide tax residence

Under Article 9.1 of the Personal Income Tax Act (IRPF), a person is resident in Spain for a calendar year if they spend more than 183 days here during that year, or if the main centre or base of their activities or economic interests is in Spain. Sporadic absences count as days in Spain unless tax residence in another country is proved. The card, the NIE and the date of the authorisation are not part of the test.

The same article adds a presumption, which admits proof to the contrary. The presumption applies to a person whose spouse, not legally separated, and minor children who depend on them both habitually live in Spain. It does not apply to unmarried partners, to parents or adult children, or where only one of the two requirements is met. For those, only the 183-day and economic-interest tests apply. Where the presumption does apply, it can be rebutted, and if another country also treats the person as resident, the double taxation treaty decides. See Tax residence when moving to Spain.

Worldwide income and assets abroad

A resident pays Spanish income tax on all income, wherever it is obtained and wherever the payer lives (Article 2). A relative who arrives with a pension, savings or a rented home in their country of origin declares that income in Spain for every year in which they are resident. Accounts, securities, insurance and real estate held abroad may also require Form 720 (Additional Provision 18 of the General Tax Act); virtual currencies held abroad are reported separately, on Form 721. Both have their own thresholds and deadlines: see Form 720: assets abroad.

Joint or individual returns

Joint taxation is available only to the family unit defined in Article 82: the spouses not legally separated and their minor children (except those living independently with their parents’ consent) or, in cases of legal separation or no marriage, one parent with the children; the other parent files individually. Unmarried partners therefore cannot file a single joint return together. The composition of the unit is taken on 31 December of each year, and all members must be IRPF taxpayers.

Article 83 adds the condition that decides most first years: every member of the unit must be an IRPF taxpayer. If the relative arriving from abroad is not resident in Spain for that year, the family cannot file jointly and each resident member files individually. Whether joint filing is better is then a matter of figures, checked each year.

When the relative starts working

If the relative is an IRPF taxpayer (tax resident) that year, Article 96 exempts from filing where the only income is from employment up to 22,000 euros a year, or 15,876 euros where there is more than one payer and the second and later payers together exceed 1,500 euros, among other cases. A resident registered as self-employed at any time during the year, or entitled to the deduction for international double taxation, must file in every case. A relative who is not resident that year does not file a Renta return. For tax purposes a foreigner’s identification number is the NIE; without one, a tax number beginning with M is requested from the Tax Agency for transactions with tax consequences (Article 20 of the General Tax Management Regulation).

The special regime for relocated workers: only in specific cases

The impatriate regime, known as the Beckham Law, applies to someone who moves to Spain for one of the reasons in Article 93.1, such as an employment contract, without having been resident here in the five previous tax periods. Article 93.3 allows the spouse and children under 25, or of any age if they have a disability, of a person who opts for the regime to opt as well, if the conditions set out there are met, including that the spouse and children have not been resident in the previous five periods and that their combined taxable bases are lower than the main taxpayer’s. The time limit is in Article 116 of the IRPF Regulation: six months from their entry into Spain, or the main taxpayer’s period if that ends later.

In families of Spanish citizens this is not the usual situation: it requires one family member to be relocated under Article 93 and every condition of Article 93.3 to be checked before opting. See the Beckham Law guide. Years already spent here without filing are a separate question: see Regularising your taxes as a foreigner.

Frequently asked questions: family of Spanish citizens and tax

Does the family residence card make me tax resident in Spain?

Not by itself. Tax residence depends on Article 9.1 of the IRPF Act: more than 183 days in Spain in the calendar year or the main centre of economic interests here, with a rebuttable presumption only where the spouse, not legally separated, and the dependent minor children both live in Spain. The presumption does not apply to unmarried partners, parents or adult children.

Can we file a joint return in the year I arrive?

Only if every member of the family unit is an IRPF taxpayer for that year (Article 83). If the relative arriving from abroad is not resident in Spain that year, each resident member files individually. The condition is checked year by year, so it may be met in later years.

Can unmarried partners file jointly?

No. Without marriage, the family unit under Article 82 is formed by one parent and the children; the other parent files individually, so the two partners cannot be in the same joint return.

Can I opt for the Beckham Law as the spouse?

Only in specific cases. Under Article 93.3, the spouse and children under 25, or of any age if they have a disability, of a person who opts for the regime may opt as well if its conditions are met, including that they have not been resident in the previous five periods and that their combined taxable bases are lower than the main taxpayer’s. The time limit is in Article 116 of the IRPF Regulation: six months from their entry, or the main taxpayer’s period if later. It is not the usual situation in families of Spanish citizens.
