Arraigo in Spain: what it means for your taxes

Your tax residence in Spain is decided by the Personal Income Tax Act, not by your immigration status. Arraigo is summarised briefly below; the rest of this guide covers what decides your tax position before and after the permit.

Arraigo in brief

Arraigo provides routes to temporary residence on exceptional grounds: segunda oportunidad, sociolaboral, social, socioformativo and familiar, under RD 1155/2024 as amended in April 2026. Time on the municipal register alone is insufficient: each route has general conditions and its own evidence. The general rule requires two years of continuous presence before applying; familiar has no minimum presence requirement.

For sociolaboral, formal admission for processing, recorded in the commencement notice, provisionally authorises residence and employed work until the decision; merely registering an application is insufficient. Initial validity is one year, except familiar, which is five years, and an extension is not automatic. New applications under the 2026 extraordinary routes closed on 30 June 2026, as checked on 21 October 2026.

Three rules that decide your tax position

Residence, year by year

More than 183 days in the calendar year, or your main centre of economic interests in Spain. No permit is required.

IRPF Act · Art. 9.1

Worldwide income

A resident is taxed on all income, wherever it arises and wherever the payer is based.

IRPF Act · Art. 2

Who must file

A resident employee below the thresholds may be exempt from filing; a resident registered as self-employed during the year files in every case.

IRPF Act · Art. 96

The permit does not decide tax residence

Article 9.1 of the Personal Income Tax Act (IRPF) makes you resident in Spain for a calendar year if you spend more than 183 days here during that year, or if the main centre or base of your activities or economic interests is in Spain. Sporadic absences count as days in Spain unless you prove tax residence in another country. Neither test refers to a NIE, a TIE, a residence authorisation or the municipal register.

Someone who has completed two years of continuous presence will normally have spent more than 183 days in Spain in at least one calendar year, and so will often have been tax resident here even without a permit. Each year has to be checked on its own days and economic ties. If another country also treats you as resident, the double taxation treaty between the two countries decides which one prevails for the treaty’s purposes. Tax residence when moving to Spain explains the tests in detail.

Residence means worldwide income

A resident pays Spanish income tax on all income, regardless of where it is obtained and where the payer lives (Article 2). Rent from a flat in your home country, interest on an account abroad or a foreign pension belongs in the Spanish return for each year in which you are resident, not only income earned in Spain.

Accounts, securities, insurance and real estate held abroad may also trigger the information return on assets abroad, Form 720 (Additional Provision 18 of the General Tax Act); virtual currencies held abroad are reported separately, on Form 721. It is a separate obligation from the income tax return, with its own thresholds and deadlines: see Form 720: assets abroad.

When you start working or register as self-employed

Article 96 sets out which IRPF taxpayers do not have to file. A person whose only income is from employment does not file up to 22,000 euros a year; the limit falls to 15,876 euros where there is more than one payer and the second and later payers together exceed 1,500 euros, among other cases.

The exemption does not apply to everyone. If you are an IRPF taxpayer (tax resident) that year and have been registered as self-employed with Social Security at any time during it, you must file in every case, as must a taxpayer entitled to the deduction for international double taxation. A resident who starts self-employment after the permit therefore files a Renta return for that year, whatever the income. Someone who is not resident that year does not file a Renta return.

For tax purposes, a foreigner’s tax identification number is the NIE. Someone without a NIE who is going to carry out transactions with tax consequences requests from the Tax Agency a tax number beginning with M (Article 20 of the General Tax Management Regulation).

The special regime for relocated workers

The impatriate regime, known as the Beckham Law, requires not having been resident in Spain in the five tax periods before the year of the move (Article 93.1), and a move linked to an employment contract, a directorship, an entrepreneurial activity or highly qualified work for start-ups or R&D. Someone who has been living here, with or without a permit, for the two years arraigo requires will normally have been resident in some of those periods, so the regime is normally not available. Each year is checked against Article 9.1. See the Beckham Law guide.

Earlier years without returns

The order matters. First establish, year by year, whether you were resident under Article 9.1. Then check whether you had an obligation to file under Article 96, and whether foreign assets reached the Form 720 thresholds. Only then is it clear which years need a return and which do not. Years with an obligation that was not met are regularised through late returns: see Regularising your taxes as a foreigner.

Frequently asked questions: arraigo and tax

Can I be tax resident in Spain without a residence permit?

Yes. Article 9.1 of the IRPF Act looks at days spent in Spain during the calendar year and where your main centre of economic interests is; it does not require a NIE, a TIE or a permit. If you spent more than 183 days here, you are generally resident for that year.

Do I have to file a Renta return once I start working?

Not always. If you are tax resident that year, Article 96 exempts people whose only income is from employment up to 22,000 euros, or 15,876 euros where there is more than one payer and the second and later payers exceed 1,500 euros, among other cases.

Does the filing threshold apply if I register as self-employed?

No. If you are an IRPF taxpayer (tax resident) that year and have been registered as self-employed with Social Security at any time during it, you must file a Renta return for that year in every case, under Article 96. Someone who is not resident that year does not file a Renta return.

Can I use the Beckham Law after arraigo?

Normally not. The regime requires not having been resident in Spain in the five tax periods before the year of the move (Article 93.1), and a move linked to an employment contract, a directorship, an entrepreneurial activity or highly qualified work for start-ups or R&D. Someone who has been living here, with or without a permit, for the two years arraigo requires will normally have been resident in some of those periods, so the regime is normally not available. Each year is checked against Article 9.1.
