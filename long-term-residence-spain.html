Long-term residence in Spain: what happens to your taxes

Español

A long-term residence permit does not decide where you pay tax. People who reach it have usually lived in Spain for years and, under Spanish income tax rules, have been tax resident for years: they declare here what they earn worldwide, including a pension, rent or interest from their home country.

Grupo Avanza Consultores, tax and accounting adviser in Paterna (Valencia), handles the tax side of this situation: your tax residence each year, the Spanish return with foreign income, Form 720 and regularising years that were never filed. We can start remotely and reply in writing in English.

The permit, in brief

Spanish national long-term residence and EU long-term residence are two different authorisations under the general regime for third-country nationals; EU, EEA and Swiss citizens have their own regime. Both allow indefinite residence and work in Spain. EU status also requires sufficient fixed, regular resources and health insurance, and its name does not grant EU citizenship.

The ordinary route starts from five years of lawful, continuous residence. Under the ordinary continuity rules, absences of up to six consecutive months are allowed, without exceeding ten months in total within the five years; for employment-related absences, the total rises to eighteen months. For EU status, certain periods of stay for studies count at 50%, provided you hold residence status in Spain when applying. The authorisation is indefinite, but the TIE card has its own validity and renewals.

Royal Decree 1155/2024 · Articles 175, 176, 183 and 185 · Migration Ministry · Sheet 49 · Migration Ministry · Sheet 50

Your permit does not decide your tax residence

The Tax Agency does not look at which card you hold, but at where you live each calendar year. You are tax resident if you spend more than 183 days of the year in Spain, and sporadic absences are counted unless you prove tax residence in another country; you are also resident, even below that number of days, if the main centre or base of your activities or economic interests is here. If your spouse who is not legally separated and your dependent minor children habitually live in Spain, you are presumed resident too, unless proven otherwise.

Anyone with five years of lawful, continuous residence has normally been tax resident in each of them, whether or not they filed. If another country also treats you as resident, the double tax treaty between the two decides. The first year is covered in tax residence when moving to Spain.

Personal Income Tax Act (Law 35/2006) · Article 9.1

Your home-country pension and income are declared here too

A resident pays Spanish income tax on all their income, wherever it arises and wherever the payer resides. That includes the pension you receive from your country, rent from the flat you kept there, and interest and dividends from accounts and securities abroad. The treaty, if there is one, decides which country taxes each item; where both tax it, what you paid there may be deducted in Spain, within the limit of Article 80 of the Personal Income Tax Act: the lower of two amounts is deducted, the tax paid abroad or the result of applying the effective average tax rate to the part of the tax base taxed abroad.

That entitlement also affects whether you must file. With employment income only, the general limit is €22,000, but it falls to €15,876 if there is more than one payer and the second and later payers exceed €1,500, or if the payer is not obliged to withhold. And anyone entitled to the international double taxation deduction must file in every case. There are separate guides for pensions from the UK, Germany, France and Italy.

Personal Income Tax Act · Article 2 · Article 80 · Articles 96.2, 96.3 and 96.4

Assets abroad: Form 720

As a resident, accounts, securities, insurance and real estate located outside Spain are reported on Form 720, an information return separate from the income tax return; virtual currencies held abroad are reported separately, on Form 721. Anyone who has kept the family flat or the pension account in their home country may never have reported it. Whether you must file depends on the amounts and on what you have already declared; thresholds and deadlines are in the Form 720 guide.

General Tax Act (Law 58/2003) · Additional provision eighteen

Unfiled years: residence first, then the returns

If you have been tax resident and did not file, or filed only for what you were paid in Spain, it is best to follow this order: residence is decided year by year, income from inside and outside Spain is reconstructed, and only then do you see which returns are missing and which need correcting. For the Tax Agency, your tax number (NIF) is your NIE; anyone without one who is going to carry out transactions with tax consequences asks the Tax Agency for a NIF starting with M. The rest is in regularising your taxes in Spain as a foreigner.

The special regime for relocated workers, known as the Beckham Law, requires not having been resident in Spain in the five tax periods before the move. If you were resident in any of the five periods before the move, you do not meet the condition in Article 93.1.a. The conditions are in the Beckham Law guide.

Royal Decree 1065/2007 · Article 20 · Personal Income Tax Act · Article 93.1.a

Time abroad and leaving Spain

The fact that your permit tolerates certain absences says nothing about your taxes: tax residence is measured again each year under Article 9. Someone who spends long periods in their home country may remain tax resident, through family or economic interests, and someone who settles abroad may stop being resident while still holding the card. If you stop being a Spanish income taxpayer because you change residence, income pending allocation is included in the last return you must file; if you move to another EU Member State, you may opt to file supplementary returns as that income arises. If you then keep property or income in Spain, you move to Modelo 210 for non-residents. The rest is in leaving Spain.

Personal Income Tax Act · Article 14.3

Questions about long-term residence and tax

Does long-term residence make me tax resident in Spain?

Not by itself. Tax residence is decided by Article 9 of the Personal Income Tax Act, calendar year by calendar year: more than 183 days in Spain, counting sporadic absences unless you prove tax residence in another country, or the main centre of your activities or economic interests being here. If your spouse who is not legally separated and your dependent minor children habitually live in Spain, you are presumed resident too, unless proven otherwise. Anyone reaching long-term residence has normally met this for years.

Do I have to declare in Spain the pension or rent I receive in my home country?

If you are tax resident, yes: Spanish income tax applies to all your income, wherever it arises and wherever the payer resides (Article 2). The double tax treaty, if there is one, decides which country taxes each item. If you are entitled to the international double taxation deduction, you must file a return even if your income is below the limits that generally exempt you from filing (Article 96.4).

Do I have to file Form 720 for my flat or bank account in my home country?

Form 720 is the information return for assets and rights located abroad: accounts, securities, insurance and real estate (additional provision eighteen of the General Tax Act); virtual currencies held abroad are reported separately, on Form 721. Whether you must file depends on the amounts and on what you declared in previous years; thresholds and deadlines are in our Form 720 guide.

If I spend long periods outside Spain, do I stop being tax resident?

It depends on each calendar year, not on your permit: days are counted, sporadic absences are included unless you prove tax residence in another country, and your economic interests and where your family lives carry weight. If you stop being a taxpayer because you change residence, income pending allocation is included in your last Spanish income tax return (Article 14.3).
