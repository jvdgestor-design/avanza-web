Family reunification: your family’s taxes in Spain

Español

A family reunification authorisation lets your family live in Spain; it does not decide who is tax resident or from when. The procedure is summarised briefly below; the rest of this guide covers each member’s residence, the arrival year, joint returns and the special regime for relocated workers.

The procedure in brief

General family reunification allows a non-EU foreign resident to bring certain relatives to Spain. As a general rule, the sponsor must have lived in Spain for at least one year and applied for authorisation for at least another year; to bring parents, the sponsor needs long-term or long-term EU residence granted in Spain. Relatives of Spanish citizens and of EU, EEA or Swiss citizens have their own regimes.

Article 66 of the Immigration Regulation sets out which relatives may join (spouse or partner, children under 18, certain adult children and parents, each under its conditions), and Article 67 requires sufficient resources, adequate housing and healthcare cover. The general benchmark for resources is 150% of the IPREM (the Spanish public income indicator) for the sponsor and one relative, plus 50% for each additional member. The authorisation for a spouse, partner or child of working age allows employed or self-employed work (Article 65.2).

Organic Law 4/2000 · Art. 18.1 · RD 1155/2024 · Arts. 65–68 · Ministry Information Sheet 8 (May 2025)

Three rules that decide the family’s taxes

Each person, each year

Tax residence is assessed person by person and calendar year by calendar year: more than 183 days in Spain or the centre of economic interests here.

IRPF Act · Art. 9.1

The family presumption

If your spouse, not legally separated, and the minor children who depend on you live here, you are presumed resident too. Proof to the contrary is admitted.

IRPF Act · Art. 9.1

Joint return

Only for the family unit at 31 December, and only if every member is an IRPF taxpayer that year.

IRPF Act · Arts. 82–83

The sponsor: the family strengthens their residence

Under Article 9.1 of the Personal Income Tax Act (IRPF), a person is resident in Spain if they spend more than 183 days here in the calendar year, counting sporadic absences unless tax residence in another country is proved, or if the main centre or base of their activities or economic interests is in Spain. The same article adds a presumption, which admits proof to the contrary: a person is presumed resident if their spouse, not legally separated, and the minor children who depend on them habitually reside in Spain.

When the family arrives, that presumption adds to the sponsor’s days and economic interests. If the sponsor keeps a business or a home in their country of origin, the family’s arrival makes it harder to maintain that their tax residence is abroad. As a resident they declare their worldwide income: everything they obtain, wherever it arises and wherever the payer lives (Article 2). If another country also treats them as resident, the double taxation treaty decides. The NIE, the TIE and the date of the authorisation are not part of this test.

Each joining relative, separately

The tax residence of the spouse and children who arrive is assessed under the same criteria, for each person and each year. The delicate year is the year of arrival: a relative who enters Spain in the second half of the year may not exceed 183 days and may not yet have their centre of interests here. For someone joining a sponsor who already lives here, the Article 9.1 presumption may also count, but it does not replace the days or the interests; what decides is the evidence for that particular year.

From the year in which they are resident, the relative declares their worldwide income in Spain: a pension, a salary or the rent from the home they keep in their country. Accounts, securities, insurance and real estate held abroad may also require Form 720 (Additional Provision 18 of the General Tax Act), with its own thresholds and deadlines; virtual currencies held in custody abroad are reported separately, on Form 721. See Form 720: assets abroad. If they are not resident in the arrival year, any income they obtain in Spain is treated as non-resident income: see Modelo 210. The arrival date and its consequences are covered in Tax residence when moving to Spain.

Joint return: the arrival year is usually excluded

Joint taxation is available only to the family unit defined in Article 82: spouses not legally separated and their minor children or, without marriage, one parent with the children. The members of an unmarried couple do not form a family unit with each other: only one of them can form one with the children living with them; the other files individually. The composition of the unit is taken on 31 December of each year.

Article 83 adds the condition that decides the first year: every member of the unit must be an IRPF taxpayer. If the spouse or a child who arrives through family reunification is not resident in Spain that year, the family cannot file jointly and each resident member files individually. From the year in which everyone is resident, a joint return is an option to be compared against the figures.

When the relative has to file

Article 96 exempts from filing a person whose only income is from employment up to 22,000 euros a year, or 15,876 euros where there is more than one payer and the second and later payers together exceed 1,500 euros, among other cases. Anyone registered as self-employed at any time during the year, or entitled to the deduction for international double taxation, must file in every case (Article 96.4). For tax purposes a foreigner’s identification number is the NIE; someone who does not yet have one and will carry out transactions with tax consequences requests from the Tax Agency a tax number beginning with M (Article 20 of the General Tax Management Regulation).

The impatriate regime: also for spouse and children, under conditions

The special regime for relocated workers, known as the Beckham Law, applies to someone who moves to Spain for one of the reasons in Article 93 of the IRPF Act, such as an employment contract, without having been resident here in the five previous tax periods; it lasts for the year of the move and the following five. Article 93.3 allows the spouse and children under 25, or of any age if they have a disability, of a person who opts for the regime to opt as well, under the conditions it sets out. Their deadline is six months from their entry into Spain, or the main taxpayer’s period if that ends later (Article 116.1.b of the IRPF Regulation).

Two of those conditions matter in family reunification. The family must move to Spain with the main taxpayer or later, but before the end of the first tax period in which the regime applies to the main taxpayer (Article 93.3.a). And the sum of the taxable bases of the relatives who opt must be lower than the main taxpayer’s (Article 93.3.d). The other conditions are in the Beckham Law guide.

If there are years without a return, foreign income left out or a Form 720 outstanding, they are regularised by filing the corresponding returns: see Regularising your taxes as a foreigner.

Questions about family reunification and tax

Does the family reunification card make my relative tax resident?

Not by itself. Tax residence depends on Article 9.1 of the IRPF Act: more than 183 days in Spain in the calendar year or the main centre of economic interests here, with a presumption, which admits proof to the contrary, where the spouse and minor children live in Spain.

Can we file a joint return in the year my family arrives?

Only if every member of the family unit is an IRPF taxpayer for that year (Article 83). If the relative who arrives is not resident that year, each resident member files individually. The composition of the unit is taken on 31 December (Article 82).

Can my spouse opt for the Beckham Law?

Only if the person who relocated opts for the regime and the conditions of Article 93.3 are met; it covers the spouse and children under 25 or of any age with a disability. Among other conditions, they must move with the main taxpayer or later, before the end of the first tax period in which the regime applies to the main taxpayer, and the sum of their taxable bases must be lower than the main taxpayer’s. The deadline to opt is six months from their entry into Spain or the main taxpayer’s period if later (Article 116 of the IRPF Regulation).

Does my relative have to file in the first year?

If they are tax resident that year, the Article 96 rules apply: someone whose only income is employment income up to 22,000 euros, among other cases, is not required to file; anyone registered as self-employed at any time during the year must file in every case. If they are not resident, any income they obtain in Spain is treated as non-resident income.
